# SID Fit Coach — Training Journey Specification

## 1. Purpose and evidence boundary

This file adds a structured training journey to the SID Fit Coach project. It is written for one adult user: male, 27, 168 cm, approximately 87 kg, full gym access, and three core training sessions plus one optional session weekly.

The journey uses broadly supported resistance-training principles: progressive overload, sufficient weekly exposure to major movement patterns, moderate effort with repetitions in reserve, stable exercise selection long enough to observe progress, and autoregulation around recovery. Exact sets, reps, frequency, cardio dose, and exercise choices remain starting recommendations because the user’s training history, technique, injury history, occupation, sleep, and response to training are not fully known.

This is not medical rehabilitation or a diagnosis. Pain, acute symptoms, recent surgery, major medical conditions, medication-related constraints, or concerning fatigue trigger the Safety Gate.

## 3. Research-informed principles

### 3.1 Progressive resistance training

Muscle and strength adaptations depend on repeated challenging sets, adequate recovery, and progression over time. Progress does not require maximal loads in every session. A wide range of moderate repetition loads can be useful when sets are sufficiently challenging and technique is maintained. This program therefore uses mostly 6–15 repetitions, with some higher-repetition accessory work.

### 3.2 Effort and proximity to failure

Training close to failure can stimulate adaptation, but taking every set to failure increases fatigue and may reduce quality. Use RIR/RPE: early sets usually leave 2–4 repetitions in reserve; selected accessory sets may approach 1–2 repetitions in reserve when technique remains stable. Avoid failure on technically demanding compound lifts for this user’s first phase.

### 3.3 Weekly volume and frequency

There is no single universal set target. Start with a recoverable amount, distribute work across three full-body sessions, and adjust from performance, soreness, sleep, motivation, and progression. A muscle group receiving roughly 6–12 challenging weekly sets is a reasonable starting range for many newer or returning trainees, not a rule that overrides recovery. Add volume only when performance and recovery support it.

### 3.4 Exercise selection

Choose stable movements that train the major patterns and permit consistent loading: squat/leg press, hinge, horizontal push, vertical push, horizontal pull, vertical pull, loaded carry or trunk stability, and optional isolation work. The “best” exercise is the one the user can perform safely, comfortably, consistently, and progressively.

### 3.5 Warm-up

Use 5–10 minutes of general movement plus several ramp-up sets for the first demanding exercise. Warm-up sets are not counted as hard working sets. Do not turn the warm-up into exhausting cardio.

### 3.6 Cardio alongside lifting

Moderate cardio and increased daily steps can support health and energy expenditure. Keep easy cardio easy enough that it does not regularly compromise lifting quality. Separate demanding endurance work from lower-body lifting when possible. The program starts with low-to-moderate cardio and builds based on tolerance.

### 3.7 Recovery and deloading

Sleep, nutrition, stress, and life activity affect training response. A deload is not automatically required on a calendar date; use it when multiple indicators show accumulated fatigue: repeated performance decline, unusual soreness, poor sleep, persistent joint irritation, reduced motivation, or elevated perceived effort at usual loads. Reduce sets by approximately 30–50% for one week while preserving controlled movement, then resume progressively.

## 4. 12-week journey map

### Phase 1 — Foundation (Weeks 1–4)

Objective: establish attendance, technique, exercise tolerance, and a baseline for progression.

- Three full-body sessions each week.
- Two working sets for many exercises in week 1; progress toward two to three sets by weeks 2–4 if recovery is good.
- RIR 3–4 in week 1, moving toward RIR 2–3.
- No forced reps, no maximal testing, no punishment cardio.
- Steps: begin around 7,000–8,000/day average.
- Cardio: two easy sessions of 15–25 minutes if feasible.

### Phase 2 — Build (Weeks 5–8)

Objective: progress load or repetitions while preserving technique.

- Three full-body sessions.
- Main patterns usually 3 working sets; accessories 2–3 sets.
- RIR 2–3 on main lifts; RIR 1–3 on stable accessories.
- Add the optional fourth session only if recovery and schedule are stable.
- Steps: build toward 8,000–10,000/day as practical.
- Cardio: two easy sessions of 20–30 minutes if it does not impair lifting.

### Phase 3 — Consolidate (Weeks 9–12)

Objective: maintain or improve performance during continued fat loss and prepare the next training block.

- Keep successful exercises stable.
- Use a lighter week if fatigue markers accumulate; otherwise continue gradual progression.
- Main patterns generally 3 sets; do not add volume merely because the phase is advanced.
- Optional fourth session remains low intensity or targeted, not a mandatory punishment session.
- End week 12 with a review of strength, waist, bodyweight trend, adherence, recovery, and preferred exercises.

## 5. Weekly schedule

A practical schedule is Monday–Wednesday–Friday, with the optional session on Saturday or Sunday. Leave at least one day between full-body sessions when possible.

- Session A: squat pattern + horizontal push/pull + hinge accessory + trunk.
- Session B: hinge/glute pattern + vertical push/pull + single-leg or knee-flexion work + trunk.
- Session C: squat/leg press variation + incline push + pull variation + posterior-chain/accessories.
- Optional D: easy cardio, walking, mobility, or a short low-fatigue accessory session.

If only three days are available, do A/B/C without trying to “make up” D.

### Optional Session D

Choose based on recovery:

- 20–40 minutes easy cycling, incline walking, or elliptical;
- 30–45 minutes walking plus mobility;
- two to four low-fatigue accessory exercises at RIR 3;
- skip it if sleep, soreness, joint comfort, or motivation is poor.

## 7. Progression system

Use double progression with a logbook.

1. Pick a load that allows the bottom of the repetition range with the target RIR.
2. Add one repetition when form and RIR are appropriate.
3. When all sets reach the top of the range at target effort, increase the smallest practical load increment.
4. Return to the lower end of the range and rebuild.
5. If performance falls for one session, repeat the load or reduce one set; do not assume the program failed.
6. If performance falls for two or more sessions plus recovery worsens, use the fatigue decision rule.

### Example

If dumbbell press is 20 kg for 3 × 8–12:

- Week 1: 20 kg × 8, 8, 8 at RIR 3.
- Later: 20 kg × 10, 9, 9.
- When 20 kg × 12, 12, 12 is controlled at target effort, try the next manageable load and restart near 8 repetitions.

## 8. Fatigue decision rule

| Signals | Action |
|---|---|
| One poor session, sleep poor but no pain | Repeat load, remove one accessory set, reassess next session |
| Two poor sessions plus high effort and soreness | Reduce sets 30–50% for up to one week; keep technique |
| Persistent pain, swelling, loss of function, or red-flag symptoms | Stop triggering activity and seek qualified assessment; do not self-prescribe rehabilitation |
| Strength stable, waist down, scale variable | Maintain plan; body recomposition is plausible |
| Weight loss fast plus fatigue/strength decline | Do not increase cardio or cut calories; review recovery and seek professional input if persistent |

## 9. Missed-session rules

- Miss one session: continue with the next planned session; do not double volume.
- Miss a full week: restart with approximately 70–80% of the prior working volume and RIR 3, then rebuild.
- Sleep is severely reduced or the user feels acutely unwell: use walking/mobility or rest rather than forcing a hard session.
- A busy week is not a failure; the minimum successful week is two short full-body sessions plus normal walking, then resume the plan.

## 10. Evaluation dashboard

Review weekly, but do not change the plan automatically every week.

| Metric | Collection | Success signal |
|---|---|---|
| Attendance | Completed sessions / planned sessions | At least 3 core sessions most weeks |
| Performance | Load × reps at stable technique | Stable or gradual improvement in key movements |
| Effort | RIR/RPE notes | Most work within prescribed effort |
| Weight | Consistent weigh-ins and 7-day average | Gradual downward trend, not a required daily drop |
| Waist | Weekly or biweekly consistent measure | Downward trend over several weeks |
| Steps/cardio | Daily average | Sustainable increase or stable target |
| Recovery | Sleep, soreness, energy, motivation | Adequate to complete sessions without persistent decline |
| Adherence | Nutrition and training consistency | Improvement in repeatable behaviors |

## 11. Training knowledge for the coach

When explaining an exercise, cover only what the user needs: target movement, setup, two or three execution cues, breathing/bracing cue if relevant, what a normal effort feels like, the safest reasonable substitute, and when to stop. Do not overload a simple question with anatomy lectures.

When recommending a new exercise, justify it by movement pattern, stability, comfort, progression potential, time, and user preference—not by claiming it is universally superior.

When discussing hypertrophy, strength, fat loss, or recovery, state the confidence and acknowledge individual response. Avoid exact promises such as “this will build X kg of muscle” or “this burns exactly Y calories.”

## 12. Research status

This is a research-informed synthesis of established resistance-training and behavior principles rather than a claim that one exact program is uniquely optimal. For a production knowledge base, add the project’s preferred primary sources and evidence updates as separate citations, but keep the operational instructions in Master Instruction and this file. Do not turn every user answer into a literature review.

## 13. Confidence

High confidence: progressive resistance training, gradual progression, recovery-aware autoregulation, stable exercise selection, multi-metric review, and non-punitive adherence principles.

Medium confidence: exact set ranges, exercise order, cardio dose, calorie target, and the user’s rate of body-composition change without training history and longitudinal data.
